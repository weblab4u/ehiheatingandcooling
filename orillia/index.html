What shapes heating and cooling in Orillia homes

Orillia sits where the Snowbelt meets cottage country, and the housing is a real mix. These four things steer most of our recommendations.

  • HousingCentury homes to lakeside places

    Old brick and frame houses near downtown, post-war bungalows, and homes along Lake Couchiching and Lake Simcoe each lose heat differently.

  • Old fuelsOil, electric and propane

    Enbridge Gas covers much of town, but plenty of older and rural homes still burn oil or run baseboards. That puts them in the bigger rebate bracket.

  • WinterSnowbelt cold and lake wind

    Lake-effect snow and wind off the water push a heating system harder than the GTA does. We size for that, not for a Toronto winter.

  • VentingChimneys built for old furnaces

    Many older furnaces share a masonry chimney with the water heater. Changing one appliance changes how the other vents, so we check both.

HVAC services in Orillia for four kinds of homes

The right fix for a downtown brick house is rarely the right fix for a place on the lake. Here's how we approach each one.

Century homes near downtown

Thick walls, small basements and ducts that were added long after the house was built. We keep the ducts that work and add a ductless head in the rooms they never reached.

Post-war bungalows

Often on their second or third furnace. Swapping in a 96% AFUE two-stage furnace with sidewall venting cuts gas use and noise in one go.

Homes on Lake Couchiching and Lake Simcoe

Former summer places with baseboards and window units. A ductless heat pump gives them proper winter heat and real summer cooling in one system.

Rural homes in Severn and Oro-Medonte

Oil tanks, propane and wood stoves. Let a cold-climate heat pump carry the winter while the old system waits in reserve.

Old heat, old venting: what an HVAC technician in Orillia fixes

Most Orillia calls trace back to equipment or venting that has outlived the house's needs. These are the repeat offenders, with our fix for each.

  • The oil furnace smells, and the tank is older than your kids

    Oil furnaces wear out and fuel prices move with the market. Our usual answer is a cold-climate heat pump on an electric air handler. If you're not ready to pull the tank, the oil furnace can sit in reserve for a winter first.

  • A new furnace was quoted without a word about the chimney

    A high-efficiency furnace vents through plastic pipe out the side of the house. That can leave the water heater alone in a chimney that's now too big for it, which can cause poor draft. We check it and include a liner or other fix in the quote when it's needed.

  • Baseboard heat makes January the most expensive month of the year

    Every baseboard is a straight resistance heater, so you pay full price for every bit of warmth. Heat pumps squeeze several times more warmth out of the same electricity in mild weather. And because the house is electrically heated, the rebate can reach $7,500.

  • Water drips from a pipe near the new furnace

    Condensing furnaces produce water as they run, and it needs a proper drain, trap and sometimes a small pump. We set that up on install day and check it at every tune-up.

  • The back bedrooms never warm up in a cold snap

    Older duct runs are often too small or leaky at the far end of the house. We measure airflow, seal joints and balance the dampers before deciding whether a bigger system is really needed.

  • The lake place needs a window unit in every room by July

    A multi-zone ductless system runs several quiet indoor heads from one outdoor unit. It cools the whole place in summer and heats it through the winter.

  • The electric furnace in your North Ward house eats hydro, but the ducts are fine

    Good ducts are the expensive part, and you already have them. A ducted cold-climate heat pump slides into the same duct system, with an electric air handler as the backup for the coldest nights. Because the house heats with electricity today, it sits in the higher rebate tier, and the hydro bill drops the first winter.

Frequently asked questions

Can I replace an oil furnace in Orillia with a heat pump?

Yes. The usual route is a heat pump on an electric air handler, though some Orillia owners keep the oil furnace around as a reserve for the odd bitter night. The rebate for an oil-heated house can reach $7,500, but only if pre-approval is done first.

Why does a new furnace sometimes need a chimney liner?

When a high-efficiency furnace moves its venting to the side wall, the water heater may be left alone in a chimney sized for two appliances. That can cause poor draft, so a liner or a different water heater setup is sometimes needed. If the water heater is also near the end of its life, replacing it with a power-vented or tankless model at the same time can retire the chimney entirely, and we price both routes.

Do lakeside homes on Lake Couchiching need a special heat pump?

They need a properly sized cold-climate model and good placement, not a special unit. We account for wind exposure off the lake and keep the outdoor unit clear of snow and ice.

How much should I budget for furnace installation in Orillia?

In Orillia, a new high-efficiency gas furnace begins at $3,300 installed. Your final price depends on size, venting and any chimney work, and it's locked in with a guaranteed quote before we start.

Does an Orillia HVAC contractor need to pull a permit for a furnace?

Gas appliance work has to follow Ontario's gas code and be done by a TSSA-certified technician. We look after the required permits and inspections as part of the job.

Do you serve rural homes outside Orillia?

Yes. We work in Severn, Oro-Medonte, Washago and the surrounding area, and we plan the install date with you.
