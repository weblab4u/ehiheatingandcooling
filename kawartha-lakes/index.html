Why Kawartha Lakes homeowners call us about their heat

Out here, heating trouble usually comes down to fuel, distance or old equipment. We've listed our fix for each one below.

  • The propane tank needs another fill before February is over

    A cold-climate heat pump takes over most of the heating, and the propane furnace becomes backup for the coldest nights. You'll fill far less often, and propane-heated homes may qualify for up to $7,500.

  • An old oil furnace in a Lindsay farmhouse is running on borrowed time

    One route swaps it for a ducted heat pump with an electric air handler. The other leaves it in place as backup, in a hybrid setup, for a winter or two while you get used to the new system. Either way, we size from the farmhouse's real heat loss, drafty corners included.

  • The Fenelon Falls cottage is freezing every May and October

    A ductless heat pump takes the chill off on cold spring and fall mornings and cools the place in July. Add a Wi-Fi controller and you can switch it on before you leave the city.

  • Lake snow buries everything close to the shore

    Outdoor units go on raised stands or wall brackets, away from roof edges and drift paths, so the coil keeps breathing all winter.

  • Baseboards in a Bobcaygeon house mean brutal hydro bills

    A multi-zone ductless system heats the main rooms with far less power than baseboards in most weather. Electric-heated homes also fall in the higher rebate tier.

  • You doubt a heat pump can keep up with a Kinmount winter

    The northern end of the city gets properly cold. We size the heat pump for your local design temperature and plan backup heat, from your existing furnace or an electric air handler, for nights below its rated range.

Replacing oil and propane: heat pump installation in Kawartha Lakes

Think of a cold-climate heat pump as an air conditioner that can run in reverse, gathering heat from outdoor air even well below freezing and moving it inside. Our Midea EVOX G3 and Kepler models carry a -30°C heating rating, which covers nearly all of a Kawartha winter.

For a home on oil or propane, there are two good ways to set it up. In a hybrid system, the heat pump runs most of the time and your existing furnace kicks in only during the deepest cold. In an all-electric system, a heat pump paired with an electric air handler replaces the furnace completely, and the tank can go once you're ready.

Older farmhouses and cottages without ducts get ductless heads instead, one per main area, all fed by one outdoor unit. Whatever the building, we start with a heat-loss calculation so the system isn't too small for January or too big for October.

  • Hybrid or all-electric, chosen from your fuel costs and ductwork
  • Ductless options for cottages and duct-free farmhouses
  • Panel and 240V circuit checked as ESA-licensed electrical work
  • Rebate pre-approval in place before installation

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a heat pump installed in Lindsay?

Yes. We install cold-climate heat pumps in Lindsay and across Kawartha Lakes, including Fenelon Falls, Bobcaygeon and Omemee, and we book the work on dates that suit you.

How much rebate can a propane-heated home in Kawartha Lakes get?

A propane-heated home can qualify for up to $7,500 toward a cold-climate heat pump through the Home Renovation Savings Program, depending on the heat pump's size. You need pre-approval before the work starts, and the program's terms may change.

Can I keep my oil furnace as backup?

Yes, if it's in safe working order. We connect it to the heat pump and set the switchover so it only runs in deep cold.

Is a ductless heat pump a good fit for a seasonal cottage?

Often, yes. It heats on cold spring and fall days, cools in summer and needs no ductwork, though winter use calls for decent insulation and backup heat for the coldest nights.

Do cottages qualify for the heat pump rebate?

It depends on the program terms, so we confirm eligibility for your property before installation. A year-round home on oil, propane, electric or wood heat may be eligible for as much as $7,500.

What happens to a heat pump during a power outage?

A heat pump needs electricity, so it stops during an outage, just as a furnace blower does. Many rural homes keep a wood stove or a properly installed generator as their backup plan.
