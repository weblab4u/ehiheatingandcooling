Problems a hybrid heat pump system solves

Dual fuel is the answer to a very Ontario question: how do I cut the gas bill without freezing in February?

  • Gas bills keep climbing but you don't trust all-electric at -25°C

    You don't have to choose. The heat pump carries the mild and moderate days, which is most of an Ontario winter, and the furnace carries the deep cold. Nothing about your coldest night changes except the bill.

  • Your AC died and the furnace is still good

    Swap the AC for a heat pump instead of another air conditioner. Same install footprint, and now your summer unit heats too. This is the most common hybrid job we do in Markham, Vaughan and the rest of the GTA.

  • Both the furnace and AC are at end of life

    Replace both with a matched hybrid: a Lennox ML296V or EL297V, or a Midea MGH96 furnace, with a cold-climate heat pump coil on top. One install, one warranty, two fuels.

  • Hydro and gas rates keep changing

    A hybrid thermostat switches fuel at an outdoor temperature we set. If rates shift, we move the balance point. You're never locked into one fuel.

  • You want a rebate but you heat with gas

    Enbridge gas-heated homes do get a rebate on a qualified cold-climate heat pump, just the smaller tier, commonly up to $2,000. We confirm eligibility before installation and file it for you. Rebate details here.

  • The furnace short cycles and the house swings hot and cold

    A two-stage, variable-speed furnace and an inverter heat pump both run long and low. The hybrid fixes the comfort problem, not just the fuel bill.

How a dual fuel heat pump decides which one runs

Every home has a balance point: the outdoor temperature where the heat pump's output just matches the house's heat loss. Above it, the heat pump heats the house alone, at two to three units of heat per unit of electricity. Below it, the heat pump would need help, and in a hybrid that help is the gas furnace.

A dual fuel thermostat, or an outdoor temperature sensor wired to the furnace control, handles the switch. We set the economic balance point from your heat loss, the heat pump's capacity at -15°C, and current Ontario gas and hydro rates. Below that temperature the thermostat shuts the heat pump off and runs the furnace. The two never run at the same time, because the heat pump coil sits in the airstream above the furnace heat exchanger. In practice, most Southern Ontario homes run on the heat pump for the large majority of the heating season and burn gas only through cold snaps.

Add a heat pump to your furnace, or replace both?

Two routes to the same hybrid heating system. The age and blower type of your furnace decide which one makes sense.

Add a heat pump to your existing furnace

Works when the furnace is under about 10 to 12 years old, in good condition and has a multi-speed or variable-speed blower. We replace the AC with a cold-climate heat pump condenser and matched coil, add the dual fuel control, and you're hybrid. From $5,500 installed.

Learn more

Replace both with a matched system

Lennox ML296V (96% AFUE, two-stage, variable-speed, 45k to 110k BTU) or EL297V (97% AFUE), or Midea MGH96 (96.5% AFUE, 60k to 120k BTU), paired with a Midea EVOX G3, Kepler or Lennox heat pump. Best efficiency, one warranty, and a furnace blower matched to the heat pump's airflow.

Keep the furnace, add ductless

Keep the furnace for deep cold and add a Midea Hyper Heat ductless unit for the rooms you live in. Not a true dual fuel set-up, but a budget way to cut gas use.

Frequently asked questions

What is a hybrid heat pump system?

A hybrid heat pump system, also called dual fuel, pairs an electric heat pump with a gas furnace on the same ductwork. The heat pump heats and cools for most of the year, and the thermostat switches to the furnace automatically when the outdoor temperature drops below a set balance point.

At what temperature does a hybrid system switch to the furnace?

The changeover point is set per home, usually somewhere between -5°C and -15°C depending on the heat pump's cold-weather capacity, your home's heat loss and the gas and hydro rates at the time. We set it at installation and can adjust it later.

Can I add a heat pump to my existing furnace?

Yes, if the furnace is in good condition, under about 10 to 12 years old, and has a blower that can deliver the airflow the heat pump needs. We replace the air conditioner with a cold-climate heat pump and matched coil, add a dual fuel control, and keep the furnace as backup. It starts at $5,500 installed.

Is a hybrid heat pump cheaper to run than a furnace alone?

For most Ontario homes on natural gas, yes. The heat pump delivers two to three units of heat per unit of electricity during the mild and moderate days that make up most of the heating season, and the furnace only burns gas in deep cold. Savings depend on your gas and hydro rates and where the balance point is set.

Do gas-heated homes qualify for the Ontario heat pump rebate?

Yes, at a smaller tier. Under the Home Renovation Savings Program, homes heated by Enbridge natural gas can receive a rebate on a qualified cold-climate heat pump, commonly up to $2,000, while electric, oil, propane and wood heated homes can receive up to $7,500. Pre-approval must be in place before installation, and program terms can change.

Which furnace works best with a heat pump?

A two-stage, variable-speed furnace such as the Lennox ML296V, Lennox EL297V or Midea MGH96 works best, because its blower can match the airflow the heat pump needs in both heating and cooling. Single-stage furnaces with older blowers can work but limit performance.
