Tankless water heater problems we fix

Here's what's usually behind the symptom, and what we do about it.

  • No hot water at all

    Usually one of three things: the unit isn't getting gas (closed valve, low pressure, a frozen regulator), it isn't igniting, or a sensor has locked it out. We check gas supply and pressure, the flame rod and ignitor, and the error history before replacing anything.

  • Error code on the display

    Every brand has its own list, and the common ones point to ignition failure, flame loss, overheating, a blocked vent or a fan fault. We read the code, test the part it points to, and confirm the cause rather than guessing from the number.

  • Cold water sandwich

    Hot, then a slug of cold, then hot again mid-shower. It's the gap between the unit shutting off and refiring when a tap closes and reopens. A recirculation option or a small buffer tank fixes it; sometimes a clogged inlet filter is making it worse.

  • Low flow or pressure at the taps

    Scale on the heat exchanger and a plugged inlet screen choke the flow. A descaling flush and filter clean usually restores it within an hour.

  • Scale build-up and a unit that's lost its punch

    Hard water leaves limescale inside the heat exchanger. The burner works harder, the water comes out cooler, and eventually the exchanger fails. An annual tankless water heater flush prevents the whole chain.

  • Ignition failure

    Clicking and no flame, or a flame that drops out after a few seconds. A dirty flame rod, failed ignitor, blocked intake or exhaust, or low gas pressure in a cold snap. Our TSSA-certified techs test each one in order.

  • Water leaks under the unit

    Could be a weeping relief valve, a failed heat exchanger gasket, a cracked exchanger from a freeze, or just the condensate line. We find where the water's coming from before we quote.

  • Loud, rumbling or whistling

    Rumbling usually means scale; whistling or a high-pitched fan noise points to the combustion fan or a restricted vent. Both are worth a service call before they become a breakdown.

    Describe the problem and we'll tell you what it usually is, what it costs to fix, and whether it's worth fixing.

What our annual tankless water heater maintenance includes

Rinnai and most other makers call for an annual flush, and in hard water areas that's not optional. Here's the full visit.

  • Descaling flush: 45 to 60 minutes of white vinegar or a commercial descaler circulated through the heat exchanger via the isolation valves
  • Cold water inlet filter removed and cleaned, screen checked for grit and scale
  • Air intake filter and combustion fan cleaned where fitted
  • Burner, flame rod and ignitor inspected and cleaned
  • Venting checked for blockage, sag and proper termination; condensate line and neutralizer media checked and replaced when spent
  • Gas pressure and combustion checked by a TSSA-certified technician
  • Error code history reviewed, temperature settings confirmed, relief valve tested
  • Dated service record for your warranty file

Why hard water in parts of Ontario matters for your tankless

Much of Southern Ontario sits on limestone, and the groundwater that comes with it is hard. Kitchener-Waterloo, Barrie, Newmarket and well-water homes across York, Durham and Simcoe all see it. Even Lake Ontario municipal water carries enough minerals to matter over time.

Inside a tankless unit, that water passes through a narrow heat exchanger at high temperature, which is exactly where scale likes to form. A layer of limescale insulates the exchanger, so the burner runs longer, the outlet temperature drops, flow slows, and the unit throws codes. Left long enough it cracks the exchanger, the most expensive part in the unit. Manufacturers know this, which is why the warranty expects an annual flush. On well water we also talk about a softener or a scale inhibitor ahead of the heater.

Repair or replace your tankless?

A tankless unit under ten years old with a sensor, ignitor, fan or filter problem is worth repairing. Past twelve to fifteen years, or with a cracked heat exchanger, the repair can run close to the cost of a new unit that's more efficient and carries a fresh warranty. Frequently asked questions

How often should a tankless water heater be flushed?

A tankless water heater should be flushed once a year in most of Ontario, and every six months on very hard well water. The flush circulates vinegar or a descaler through the heat exchanger for about an hour to remove limescale, and most manufacturers require it to keep the warranty valid.

Why is my tankless water heater showing an error code?

An error code means the unit has detected a fault and locked out to protect itself. The most common causes are ignition failure, flame loss, overheating from scale, a blocked vent or intake, or a fan fault. Note the code, try one reset, and if it returns, book a service call.

What is a cold water sandwich?

A cold water sandwich is the burst of cold water you feel mid-shower after a tap elsewhere closes and reopens. The tankless unit shuts off when flow stops and takes a few seconds to refire, so the water already sitting in the pipe arrives cold. A recirculation option or a small buffer tank fixes it.

Can I flush a tankless water heater myself?

You can, if the unit has isolation valves and you own a pump and hoses, but the full maintenance visit also includes cleaning the inlet filter, inspecting the burner, flame rod, fan and venting, and checking gas pressure, which needs a TSSA-certified technician. Most homeowners book the whole visit once a year.

Do you repair tankless water heaters from brands other than Rinnai?

Yes. We repair and maintain tankless water heaters of any brand across Ontario. Rinnai is our specialty as an authorized dealer and we stock common Rinnai parts, but our technicians service other makes too and source OEM parts for them.

How much does tankless water heater repair cost in Ontario?

Tankless water heater repair cost depends on the part and the brand, so we diagnose first and give a written quote before any work. Filters, flame rods and ignitors sit at the low end; combustion fans and heat exchangers cost more, and that's where we talk about repair versus a new unit from $3,300 installed.
