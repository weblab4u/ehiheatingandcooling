When Timmins cold gets the better of a heating system

In a place this cold, small design mistakes show up fast. These are the ones we fix most.

  • The heat pump can't hold the thermostat on the coldest nights

    Below its rated range, any heat pump loses ground. We size the backup heat to cover the gap, so a -35°C night means the furnace or heat strips step in and the house stays warm.

  • Backup heat kicks in and the hydro bill jumps

    Electric heat strips are simple but costly to run for long stretches. Depending on your fuel, keeping a propane, oil or gas furnace as the backup can be cheaper. We compare both for your home.

  • Drifts and roof slides bury the outdoor unit

    We set it on a raised stand or wall bracket, out from under the eaves and away from where the plow piles snow, so air can always reach the coil.

  • Defrost water freezes into a block under the unit

    Heat pumps melt frost off the coil several times a day in winter. A raised stand over a drained base lets that water get away instead of building ice that lifts and damages the unit.

  • Your system was sized by copying the old furnace label

    Old furnaces were often oversized, and a heat pump sized the same way will short cycle. We run a heat-loss calculation for your actual walls, windows and attic.

  • The oil furnace in your South Porcupine bungalow is near the end

    Replace it with a cold-climate heat pump and an electric air handler, or keep it as backup in a hybrid setup. Oil-heated homes can qualify for the higher rebate tier.

  • The thermostat has shown 'aux heat' for hours and you're not sure that's normal

    On a -35°C night, it is. The backup is doing the job it was sized for, and the heat pump will take over again when the cold eases. If the backup is running on a -10°C afternoon, the switchover point or an outdoor sensor is off, and we adjust it. We set that point from your fuel costs and heat loss, so the cheaper source runs whenever it can.

  • Sound familiar?

    Describe the problem and we'll tell you what it usually is, what it costs to fix, and whether it's worth fixing.

Hybrid heating and cooling in Timmins: why backup heat matters

A cold-climate heat pump is an electric system that pulls heat from outdoor air even in serious cold. The Midea EVOX G3 and Kepler units we install are rated to heat down to -30°C. That covers most of a Timmins winter, but not all of it.

A hybrid system closes that gap. The heat pump runs for the bulk of the season, and when the temperature falls past the point where it can keep up, a furnace or electric heat takes over automatically. You stay warm, and you burn fuel only on the nights that truly need it.

  • Switchover point set from your heat loss and fuel costs
  • Backup sized to carry the house on its own if needed
  • Existing furnace kept where it still has life left
  • Electric air handler option for homes leaving oil entirely

Frequently asked questions

What happens to a heat pump when it drops below -30°C in Timmins?

Below its rated range a heat pump's output falls, so backup heat takes over automatically. That's why every system we plan in Timmins includes a furnace or electric air handler sized to carry the house on the coldest nights.

Is a hybrid system or an all-electric heat pump better for Timmins?

It depends on your current fuel and costs. Homes with a furnace still in good shape often do best with a hybrid, while homes leaving oil entirely often choose a heat pump with an electric air handler.

How high should a heat pump sit off the ground in Timmins?

High enough to clear your typical snow depth and drifts, which we check on site. Most installs here use a raised stand or wall bracket, placed away from roof edges.

Can I get a heat pump rebate in Timmins if I heat with oil?

Yes. Oil-heated homes can qualify for up to $7,500 toward a cold-climate heat pump through the Home Renovation Savings Program, based on size. The pre-approval has to be in place before we install, and the program's terms may change.

Do you do heat pump installation in Timmins?

Yes. Our crews install cold-climate heat pumps and hybrid systems in Timmins and nearby communities, and we schedule the work with you directly.

Can you keep my existing furnace as the backup?

Often, yes. If your furnace is in good working order, we can connect it to the heat pump as backup and set the switchover point, which keeps the upfront cost down.

How much does a hybrid heat pump system cost in Timmins?

The heat pump side starts from $5,500 installed and a new high-efficiency furnace from $3,300, before any rebate. If your existing furnace is sound, it can serve as the backup, which keeps the total down. Installed pricing depends on size and installation requirements, and the quote is guaranteed in writing.
