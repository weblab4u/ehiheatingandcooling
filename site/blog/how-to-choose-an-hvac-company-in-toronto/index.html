How to Choose an HVAC Company in Toronto

Published October 2026 · Written by the EHI Heating And Cooling team · Reviewed against current TSSA, ESA and Home Renovation Savings Program rules

A good HVAC company in Toronto holds the right licences, sends its own crew, sizes equipment from a heat-loss calculation, puts one guaranteed price in writing and handles the permits and rebate paperwork without being asked. This guide shows you how to check each of those things, what to ask before you sign, which warning signs to walk away from, and what furnace, AC and heat pump work actually costs in a Toronto house.

What a licensed HVAC company in Toronto must have

HVAC in Ontario is regulated work, and the licences are easy to check once you know where to look. A company that is vague about any of these is telling you something.

TSSA registration for gas work. Anyone who connects, services or vents a gas furnace, boiler or water heater must hold a gas technician certificate from the Technical Standards and Safety Authority, and the company itself must be registered with TSSA as a fuels contractor. Ask for the technician's certificate class and the company's registration, and check the contractor on TSSA's public search before the visit.

Licensed refrigeration technicians. Air conditioners and heat pumps hold refrigerant under pressure, and Ontario requires certification to handle it. If the person brazing your line set and weighing in the charge is not certified, the warranty on the equipment is often void from day one.

ESA and ECRA for electrical. A heat pump needs a dedicated 240V circuit. That is electrical contracting, which means an Electrical Contractor Registration Agency licence and a notification of work filed with the Electrical Safety Authority. You can look up any licensed electrical contractor on ESA's website. If a salesperson says the installer will "just tie it in," ask who holds the licence.

WSIB and liability insurance. Ask for a WSIB clearance certificate and a certificate of liability insurance naming the company. The clearance can be verified online. Without these, an injury on your property or a flooded basement from a bad condensate drain becomes your problem.

None of this takes more than fifteen minutes to confirm, and a legitimate company will hand it over before you ask twice.

The 7 questions to ask before you sign

Every quote in Toronto should survive these seven questions. Write the answers down, because you will compare them later.

  1. Can I have the quote in writing with model numbers? "A 3 ton Lennox" is not a model number. You need the exact outdoor and indoor model so you can check the efficiency ratings and whether the heat pump is on NRCan's qualified products list.
  2. Who will actually do the install? The person quoting may not be the person installing. Ask whether the crew is employed by the company or subcontracted, and who holds the TSSA and refrigeration certificates on install day.
  3. Which permits and notifications does this job need, and who files them? Electrical notification to ESA at minimum for a heat pump. The answer should be "we do," with the cost in the quote.
  4. How did you size this? The right answer involves a heat-loss calculation, often called Manual J, that accounts for your walls, windows, attic and orientation. "Same size as the old one" is the wrong answer in a city full of oversized furnaces.
  5. Who registers the warranty, and what are the terms? Most extended warranties require registration within a set window and annual maintenance. Ask who registers it and what parts and labour coverage you get in years two through twelve.
  6. Will you handle the rebate pre-approval before installation? The Home Renovation Savings Program requires pre-approval before the install, and an unregistered or unqualified model gets nothing. The company should confirm eligibility and submit the application, not leave it to you after the fact.
  7. What happens if the price changes? Ask what is excluded and what would trigger an extra charge. A guaranteed price on a written scope means the number holds unless you change the scope.

Red flags that should end the conversation

Toronto has plenty of good HVAC companies and a steady supply of the other kind. These signs separate them.

  • A firm price over the phone without a visit. Nobody can size a system for a Leslieville semi or a Don Mills split-level from the kitchen phone. A phone estimate is a lure; the real number appears on install day.
  • "This price is only good today." Equipment prices do not change overnight. Deadline pricing exists to stop you getting a second quote.
  • No permit, or "you don't need one." Skipping the ESA notification or a required City of Toronto permit saves the contractor time and leaves you with an uninspected installation that can complicate insurance and resale.
  • The pitch keeps drifting to a rental. Rental furnaces and heat pumps can cost two or three times the purchase price over the term and sit as a lien on your title. If you did not ask about renting, ask why they keep bringing it up.
  • Cash only, or a large deposit before any paperwork. A deposit is normal once there is a signed contract. Cash-only pricing usually means no warranty trail and no WSIB.
  • No model numbers on the quote. It makes comparison impossible and lets a lower-tier unit arrive on the truck.
  • Scare tactics during a repair call. "Your heat exchanger is cracked, shut it off now" is a serious claim that should come with a photo or a combustion reading, not just a replacement quote.

What HVAC work typically costs in Toronto

Prices move with the size of the house, the state of the ducts and what the electrical panel can take, which is why every reputable company quotes after a visit. These are the starting points we publish and stand behind.

  • Furnace repair: quoted in writing after diagnosis. Common parts such as ignitors, flame sensors and capacitors are typically a few hundred dollars all in. Blower motors, inducer motors and control boards cost more.
  • High-efficiency furnace, installed: from $3,300 for a 96% to 97% AFUE two-stage, variable-speed Lennox or Midea unit. Larger homes and venting changes push the price up from there.
  • Cold-climate heat pump, installed: from $5,500 for a ducted Midea EVOX G3 or Kepler system rated to -30°C, before rebates. Multi-zone ductless and panel upgrades add to that.
  • AC repair: quoted after diagnosis. A capacitor or contactor is a modest repair; a compressor or coil on an old unit is where replacement enters the conversation.
  • Rinnai tankless water heater, installed: from $3,300.

Two rules protect you. First, installed pricing depends on size and installation requirements, so treat any number given without a site visit as a guess. Second, insist on a written, guaranteed quote. If the number can move after you sign, it is not a quote. See our Toronto furnace repair and Toronto AC repair pages for what each visit includes.

How Toronto's homes change the answer

The right HVAC company for a Toronto house understands that the house is the hard part. Four things come up over and over.

Century semis and row houses. Solid brick walls, little attic insulation and ductwork laid out for gravity heat mean these homes lose heat fast and move air badly. A company that quotes from square footage will oversize the furnace and undersize the returns. Ask how they plan to get cooling to the third floor, because the honest answer in Riverdale or the Annex is often a ductless head, not a bigger central unit.

Tight lots and laneways. With a metre or less beside the house, the outdoor unit's location is a design decision. Wall brackets, rear-yard placement and flat-roof mounts all have rules. A good company settles placement, noise and the neighbour's window on the site visit, and knows Toronto's noise bylaw covers stationary equipment.

Post-war bungalows and split-levels. Scarborough, Etobicoke and North York homes from the 1950s to the 1980s often have the original ducts and a furnace sized for a house twice the size. Static pressure testing matters more here than in a new build.

Condo townhomes and low-rise condos. The board or property manager usually needs to approve outdoor equipment. Companies that work in Toronto regularly will prepare the placement request rather than leave you to argue it. Many of these homes heat with electric baseboards, which puts them in the top rebate tier for a heat pump installation in Toronto.

Rebates and financing in Toronto

The main program in 2026 is the Home Renovation Savings Program, delivered through Save on Energy and Enbridge Gas with support from the Ontario government. For Toronto homeowners the key points are:

  • Cold-climate air-source heat pumps qualify for up to $7,500, with the amount based on heat pump size. Homes heated by electricity, oil, propane or wood get the higher tier. Homes heated with Enbridge natural gas get a smaller amount, commonly up to $2,000.
  • The heat pump must be on NRCan's qualified products list. Ask the company to show you the listing for the exact model on the quote.
  • Pre-approval must be in place before installation. A company that installs first and "sorts out the rebate later" has cost you the rebate.
  • No home energy assessment is needed for a heat pump on its own. A smart thermostat adds $100.
  • Confirm eligibility before installation, because program terms can change.

Toronto Hydro customers can also look at time-of-use or ultra-low overnight rate plans, which change the running-cost math for a heat pump. A company that understands your hydro and gas bills should be willing to walk through that with you.

On financing, ask whether the company offers its own plans or routes you to a lender. We offer 0% interest for 3, 6 or 9 months and open loans from 2.99% on approved credit, with rates subject to lender approval. Details are on our rebates and financing pages.

A checklist to take to every quote

Print this or keep it on your phone. If a company cannot tick most of these on the first visit, move on.

  • TSSA fuels contractor registration and the technician's gas certificate class
  • Refrigeration certification for whoever handles the line set and charge
  • ECRA/ESA licence for the electrical work, and confirmation the ESA notification is filed
  • WSIB clearance certificate and liability insurance certificate
  • Written quote with exact indoor and outdoor model numbers
  • Heat-loss calculation shown, not just a tonnage guess
  • Placement plan for the outdoor unit, including noise and the neighbour's windows
  • Permits and notifications named, priced and handled by the company
  • Warranty terms in years for parts and labour, and who registers it
  • Rebate eligibility confirmed and pre-approval filed before the install date
  • Statement of what is excluded and what could change the price
  • Condo or townhouse approval request prepared, where it applies

Three quotes with this list beside them will tell you more than any review site. When you are ready, book a visit and we will go through every line with you.
