How to Choose an HVAC Company in Hamilton: A 2026 Homeowner's Guide

Published October 2026 · Written by the EHI Heating And Cooling team · Reviewed against current TSSA, ESA and Home Renovation Savings Program rules

A good HVAC company in Hamilton is registered with the TSSA, sends licensed technicians rather than a crew you've never heard of, prices the job in writing with model numbers, and knows the difference between a double-brick house in Kirkendall and a 2015 build in Binbrook. This guide walks through how to check the licences, the seven questions to ask before you sign, the red flags, what the work typically costs, and how Hamilton's housing changes the right answer.

What a licensed HVAC company in Hamilton must have

Ontario regulates the three trades that touch a furnace, air conditioner or heat pump. Any company quoting work in Hamilton should be able to show all of the following without hesitating.

TSSA registration for gas work. Every contractor that installs or repairs a gas furnace, boiler or water heater must be registered with the Technical Standards and Safety Authority, and the technician on site must hold a TSSA gas certificate. You can confirm a contractor's registration through the TSSA's online contractor lookup, and you can ask the technician to show the certificate card.

Licensed refrigeration technicians. Anyone opening the refrigerant circuit on an air conditioner or heat pump needs the Ontario refrigeration and air conditioning mechanic trade qualification, plus the ozone depletion prevention card for handling refrigerant. Ask who will be connecting the line set and whether that person holds the licence.

ESA and ECRA for the electrical. A heat pump needs a dedicated 240V circuit. In Ontario that work has to be done by a licensed electrical contractor registered with the Electrical Contractor Registration Agency, and the job needs a notification filed with the Electrical Safety Authority. The ESA has a public lookup for licensed contractors. If the HVAC company says their installer will just wire it in, ask who holds the ECRA licence.

WSIB coverage and liability insurance. Ask for a current WSIB clearance certificate and a certificate of liability insurance. Without them, an injury on your property or damage to your home can land on you. Both are a quick email for a legitimate company.

Checking all four takes about fifteen minutes. A company that gets prickly about being asked has told you something useful.

The 7 questions to ask before you sign

Quotes in Hamilton vary by thousands of dollars for what looks like the same job. These questions find out what you're actually buying.

  1. Can I have the quote in writing with model numbers? A quote that says "high-efficiency furnace" or "3 ton heat pump" with no manufacturer and model number lets the installer swap in whatever is on the truck that week. Insist on the exact model, the size, the efficiency rating and the warranty term on paper.
  2. Who does the install? Ask whether the people who show up are the company's own licensed staff or a subcontracted crew. Both can do good work, but you want to know who to call if something goes wrong a year later.
  3. Who files the permits and notifications? Gas work, electrical work and some venting changes need paperwork with the TSSA, the ESA or the City of Hamilton. Ask which ones apply to your job and who is responsible for filing them.
  4. How did you size this? The right answer involves a heat-loss or heat-gain calculation (Manual J is the common name) based on your home's walls, windows, insulation and orientation. "Same size as the old one" is not an answer, and "square footage divided by 600" is barely one.
  5. Who registers the warranty? Many manufacturers only give the extended parts term if the equipment is registered within a set window after installation. Ask whether the company registers it in your name and sends you the confirmation.
  6. Will you handle the rebate pre-approval? The Home Renovation Savings Program requires pre-approval before the heat pump goes in. A company that installs first and says "apply after" has cost you the rebate.
  7. What happens if the price changes? Ask what is and isn't included, and whether the number on the quote is the number on the final invoice. Guaranteed pricing on the agreed scope should be in writing. If the company won't commit, assume there will be extras.

Red flags that should end the conversation

Some warning signs are obvious. Others sound reasonable until you've been burned once.

  • A price over the phone without a visit. Nobody can size a system for a 1920s house in Crown Point or a Waterdown two-storey from a phone call. A phone number is either a guess or a lowball to get in the door.
  • "This price is only good today." Equipment prices don't change overnight. Pressure to sign on the spot is a sales tactic, not a market condition.
  • No permit, no notification. If the company says a permit isn't needed for a gas or electrical change and can't explain why, walk away. Unpermitted work can surface when you sell the house or file an insurance claim.
  • A hard push to rent instead of buy. Rental furnaces, water heaters and heat pumps have their place for some homeowners, but a company that steers every conversation to a monthly payment is usually earning more from the contract than from the equipment. Ask for the total cost over the term and the buyout schedule.
  • Cash only, or a large deposit before any paperwork. A modest deposit on a signed, itemized quote is normal. Most of the money up front, with no written scope, is not.
  • No model numbers. The most common way a low quote turns into a disappointing install.
  • Nobody looked at the electrical panel. A heat pump quote that never mentions your panel, your service size or a 240V circuit was written by someone who didn't look.

What HVAC work typically costs in Hamilton

Real numbers depend on your house, so treat these as a frame for comparing quotes rather than a price list.

Furnace repair. A diagnostic visit plus a common part such as an ignitor, flame sensor, capacitor or pressure switch is usually a few hundred dollars. Blower motors, inducer motors and control boards cost more. A trustworthy company states the diagnostic fee when you book and gives you the repair price in writing before the work begins.

Furnace replacement. A high-efficiency two-stage, variable-speed gas furnace starts from $3,300 installed. The installed price climbs with BTU size, venting changes and any ductwork that needs correcting. A chimney-vented furnace in a century home that moves to direct venting through the wall is more work than a swap in a 2010 subdivision home.

AC repair. Like furnace repair, this is quoted after diagnosis. Capacitors and contactors are modest; compressors and evaporator coils are the point where a replacement quote belongs beside the repair.

Cold-climate heat pump. A ducted cold-climate heat pump starts from $5,500 installed, before any rebate. The number goes up with tonnage, electrical work and whether an air handler or furnace coil is needed. Ductless pricing depends on how many indoor heads you need and where the line sets run, so it is quoted after a visit.

Tankless water heater. A condensing Rinnai unit starts from $3,300 installed, with venting and gas line sizing driving the rest.

If a quote comes in far below these starting points, ask what was left out. If it comes in far above, ask what you're getting for the difference. Either way, a written quote with model numbers is the only thing you can compare.

How Hamilton's homes change the answer

Hamilton is not one housing market, and the right HVAC company knows which one you're in.

Century brick homes in the lower city. Westdale, Kirkendall, Locke Street and Crown Point are full of double-brick houses with plaster walls, hot-water radiators and, at best, a few undersized ducts from an early furnace conversion. A company that quotes central air for one of these without measuring duct static pressure is going to leave you with a frozen coil and a noisy, weak system. The honest options are ductless or multi-zone ductless heat pumps, a small-duct high-velocity system, or a hybrid that keeps the boiler. Ask any company you're considering how many radiator homes they've worked on and what they'd do with yours.

Post-war homes on the Mountain. Bungalows and side splits from the 1950s to 1970s usually have ducts, but they were sized for a big single-stage furnace. The Mountain also gets more wind and holds snow longer than the lower city, so outdoor units and vent terminations need to be placed with that in mind. A heat-loss calculation for a Mountain home should use a tougher design condition than the same house would get below the escarpment.

Newer builds in Stoney Creek, Ancaster, Binbrook and Waterdown. These homes have proper ductwork and builder-grade equipment that starts failing between 10 and 15 years. The decision here is usually repair versus a hybrid heat pump that replaces the AC and takes most of the heating load off the furnace. Ask for both numbers.

Humidity below the escarpment. Lake air sits over the lower city all summer. An oversized air conditioner cools fast and shuts off before it has dried the air, which is why so many lower-city homes feel clammy with the AC running. Any company sizing cooling for your house should talk about run time and moisture removal, not just tons.

Our Hamilton HVAC page goes deeper on each of these, and the heat pump installation, furnace repair and AC repair pages cover what each job looks like in this city.

Rebates and financing for Hamilton homeowners

The main rebate in 2026 is the Home Renovation Savings Program, delivered by Save on Energy and Enbridge Gas with support from the Ontario government. For a cold-climate air-source heat pump, the amount depends on the size of the unit and on how your home is heated today. Homes heated by electricity, oil, propane or wood can receive up to $7,500. Homes heated with Enbridge natural gas get a smaller amount, commonly up to $2,000. Ground-source systems can receive up to $12,000, and a smart thermostat adds $100.

Three rules matter when you're comparing companies. The heat pump has to be on Natural Resources Canada's qualified products list. Pre-approval has to be in place before installation, not after. And no home energy assessment is needed for a heat pump on its own. Program terms can change, so confirm eligibility before installation and ask the company to show you the current rules rather than quote them from memory.

In Hamilton your hydro comes from Alectra Utilities and your gas from Enbridge. That matters for two reasons: a gas-heated home lands in the smaller rebate tier, and a heat pump adds load to a hydro service that may need a panel or service upgrade through Alectra. A good quote addresses both before you sign.

On financing, look for plain terms. We offer 0% interest for 3, 6 or 9 months, or open loans from 2.99% on approved credit, with rates subject to lender approval. Whatever company you choose, get the financing terms in writing beside the cash price and ask what the total repayment is, not just the monthly figure.

A checklist to take to every quote

Print this, or keep it on your phone. Tick each item off before you sign anything.

  • TSSA contractor registration confirmed through the TSSA lookup
  • Technician's TSSA gas certificate and refrigeration licence confirmed
  • ECRA-licensed electrical contractor named for any 240V or panel work
  • WSIB clearance certificate and liability insurance received
  • Written quote with manufacturer, model numbers, size and efficiency ratings
  • Heat-loss or heat-gain calculation done on site, not from square footage
  • Duct static pressure measured if the system uses existing ducts
  • Electrical panel and service size checked for a heat pump
  • Permits and notifications listed, with who files each one
  • Rebate pre-approval filed before the install date
  • Warranty term in writing, and who registers it
  • Price guaranteed on the agreed scope, with exclusions listed
  • Financing terms and total repayment shown beside the cash price
  • After-hours contact number for the first winter and summer

If a company checks every box, you've found one worth hiring. If it misses three or four, keep looking. Hamilton has enough good HVAC companies that you don't have to settle.
